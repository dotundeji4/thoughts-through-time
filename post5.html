Human Nature

The Early Observer

The Dawn of Human Thinking

September 22, 2026 · 15 min read
◈

Series · Part 2

If you are reading this and are able to understand it, even at the most basic level, you already have some understanding of language, comprehension, and meaning. The ability as humans to read, speak, and understand is part of our human biology. But although the ability to learn language is biological, the language itself is something we learn through experience.

A baby is born into this world.

For the very first time, the baby is held by its mother, crying. Taking in a breath of fresh air, this is, unknowingly to the baby, one of its very first experiences of being human.

The baby is an observer.

Its eyes are wide open as it sees the world around it. It observes, reacts, experiences. It does not yet understand the world in the same way that an adult does. It does not yet have a developed concept of good or evil, success or failure, right or wrong.

But the baby does have something.

Biology.

It has hunger. It has reflexes. It has the ability to form attachments. It has certain biological tendencies that do not need to be taught.

The baby is not a completely blank slate.

But much of what the baby eventually comes to understand about the world must be learned through experience.

A baby learns its first words from the people around it. It learns what certain sounds mean. It learns what makes its mother smile, what makes someone angry, what brings comfort, what causes pain. Over time, the baby begins to associate things with one another.

The world begins to become understandable.

The Order of Things

There is an order to which things happen in reality.

The Big Bang, the universe, elements, chemistry, self-replicating molecules, life, mammals, hominins, and eventually Homo sapiens.

There is an order to which things happen not only in the universe, but also within human development and human society.

Let us go back to one of the earliest periods of human civilization.

Imagine a human environment where survival is much more immediate than it is for most people today.

Food must be found. Shelter must be protected. Predators must be avoided. Children must survive. The group must survive.

Reproduction becomes important because without reproduction, the group eventually disappears.

The Body Speaks First

I do not have to consciously tell myself that I am hungry.

My body communicates that something is wrong. My stomach, nervous system, hormones, and brain work together to create the feeling we call hunger. I experience the need to eat. If I continually choose not to eat, my body becomes weaker, and eventually, if food is not obtained, death follows.

The same is true of other biological drives.

I see someone I am attracted to, and my body may respond before I have consciously decided anything. Attraction does not necessarily mean that I consciously want reproduction. Sex can be connected to pleasure, intimacy, connection, attraction, and reproduction.

But reproduction has another function. It allows life to continue. It allows our genetics to be passed forward.

Some people eat because they enjoy food. Others eat because food is necessary for survival. Some people pursue sexual intercourse for pleasure. Others see it as part of intimacy, connection, or reproduction.

The same biological systems can exist underneath very different human experiences.

This is important because there are certain aspects of what we do throughout our lives that may be rooted in human nature, while other aspects are shaped by our environment, our experiences, our circumstances, and our individual desires.

What Occupied the Early Mind?

So what occupied the human mind in these early environments?

We cannot simply know exactly what prehistoric humans were thinking about. They were not merely primitive versions of us walking around thinking only about food and sex. They had relationships. They communicated. They cooperated. They created tools. They likely experienced curiosity, play, attachment, conflict, and many other aspects of human life.

But imagine an environment where survival demands are constantly immediate.

What would occupy your mind?

Probably food. Shelter. Safety. Family. The group. Reproduction. Survival.

The mind would have strong reasons to focus its attention on the things that mattered immediately.

When the Tiger Is No Longer Outside the Cave

But then something begins to change.

Over thousands of years, humans develop increasingly complex societies. Agriculture develops. Cities develop. Technology develops. Institutions develop.

Eventually, most people in modern societies do not need to hunt an animal themselves in order to eat. You can walk into a grocery store. You can order food from your phone. You can live inside a building protected from the weather. You can turn on a light with the movement of your finger.

The human species has created an environment where many of the immediate problems of survival have been reduced.

So what happens to the mind?

Once the immediate problem is no longer "How do I survive today?" the mind can begin asking different questions.

Who am I? How do other people see me? Am I attractive? Am I successful? Do people like me? Why did they say that? Why did I say that? What will happen tomorrow?

The tiger is no longer outside the cave.

But perhaps the mind has created new things to fear.

People today can experience anxiety because of social situations. They can compare themselves to thousands of people through social media. They can worry about money, appearance, status, relationships, careers, and how they are perceived by others.

Humans cheat and feel guilty. We suffer because of things we have done. We suffer because of things that have been done to us. We fantasize about people we are attracted to while being in relationships. We replay conversations. We imagine conversations that have not happened yet.

The difference is that many of these problems are no longer directly about staying alive. They are about living. And perhaps that distinction is important.

When survival becomes less immediate, the mind does not necessarily become quiet. It may simply find new things to think about.

A person can be physically safe inside their home and still experience an internal threat. A person can have food in the refrigerator and still worry about whether they are enough. A person can be surrounded by people and still feel alone. A person can be admired by others and still question how they look in the mirror.

The Machinery That Remains

The environment changes. The human ability to think remains.

Beneath the complexity of civilization, there are still biological systems operating within us.

We are still hungry. We still seek connection. We still experience attraction. We still fear rejection. We still seek safety. We still care about our position within the group.

The environment has changed dramatically. The human being has changed with it. But some of the machinery remains.

The Observer Becomes an Individual

Now we can return to the baby.

A baby is born without an understanding of good or evil. But the baby is not born without biology. It is born with a nervous system capable of responding to the world. It is born with needs. It is born with tendencies. And then it begins to experience.

The baby does not choose its parents. It does not choose its environment. It does not choose the language it will speak. It does not choose the first people who will teach it what the world means.

The adults around the baby begin, in many ways, to introduce the world to it.

This is good. This is bad. This is safe. This is dangerous. This is love. This is normal. This is what we do. This is who you are.

The baby absorbs experiences before it can fully understand what those experiences mean. And over time, those experiences begin to form patterns.

The child learns. The child remembers. The child associates. The child begins to predict.

Eventually, the child develops a concept of self.

I am. I want. I don't want. I like this. I hate this. I am good at this. I am bad at this. They like me. They don't like me.

And something begins to happen. The observer begins to become an individual.

Where Does Choice Begin?

Initially, the developing self is heavily dependent on the world around it. The child has limited control over where it goes, what it experiences, and what information enters its mind.

But eventually, that child becomes an adult. And now something changes.

The person who was once shaped almost entirely by the decisions of others begins to make decisions of their own. The person who once observed the world begins to interpret it. The person who once learned from others begins to decide what they believe.

But even then, how much of the adult is truly independent?

If my childhood shaped my beliefs... If my biology shaped my impulses... If my environment shaped my desires... If my experiences shaped my fears... If society shaped my definition of success...

Then where exactly does my choice begin?

Maybe becoming an adult is not the moment when we suddenly become completely free. Maybe it is the moment when we become capable of observing what has shaped us.

And perhaps that is where the development of the self becomes truly interesting.

The Cycle Continues

The baby begins as an observer. It experiences. It learns. It remembers. It develops patterns. It develops a concept of itself. It becomes an individual. It becomes an adult. And eventually, that adult may become a parent.

Then the cycle begins again.

Another baby enters the world. Another observer opens its eyes. Another human being begins experiencing reality. And once again, the world begins shaping a mind.

Perhaps the story of the human being is not simply the story of survival.

Perhaps it is the story of an organism that survives long enough to become aware of itself.

And once it becomes aware of itself, it begins asking questions about the very experiences that created it.
