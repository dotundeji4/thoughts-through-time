Before and After: The Start of Everything

The Dawn of Human Thinking

September 15, 2026 · 12 min read
✦

Before the Big Bang, there is nothing.

Nothing.

Nothing.

But what is this nothing?

Close your eyes for a moment.

What do you see?

Some of you might say black. Others might say nothing. But now let us take it one step further.

Imagine nothing.

Not an empty room. Not darkness. Not outer space. Not an empty field.

Nothing.

No objects. No light. No sound. No space. No time. No matter.

What happens?

Does your mind actually become blank? Or do you find yourself thinking about what nothing would look like? Maybe you focus on your breathing. Maybe you hear the hum inside your ears. Maybe you imagine darkness.

But notice what has happened.

You were asked to imagine nothing, yet your mind immediately created something to represent it.

Perhaps this is because we have never experienced absolute nothingness.

The Problem of Before

Now imagine a time before you were born.

We can understand it conceptually. We can say, "I didn't exist yet." But can you actually remember what it felt like to not exist?

No.

You cannot remember your own nonexistence because there was no "you" there to experience it.

All you have ever known is existence.

Your existence.

Your body.

Your thoughts.

Your memories.

Your awareness moving through time.

The Absence of Things

Currently, I have nothing in my hands.

If you asked me what I have in my hands, I could simply say, "Nothing."

But notice what I mean when I say that.

I don't mean that there is a mysterious substance called "nothing" sitting inside my hands.

I mean there is an absence of something.

No phone. No object. No material possession.

So when we say "nothing," are we actually describing a thing?

Or are we describing the absence of things?

Because if absolute nothingness means the complete absence of everything, then how could something ever come from it?

We often say: "Something came from nothing."

But what exactly does that mean?

If there truly was nothing, there would be nothing there waiting.

Nothing capable of waiting.

Nothing capable of changing.

Nothing capable of becoming something.

So perhaps the question isn't simply: "What came before the Big Bang?"

Perhaps the deeper question is: "What do we even mean by before?"

And even more importantly: "What do we mean by nothing?"

Whoosh. Flashing Lights.

The Big Bang happens.

Now something is happening.

The universe begins expanding from an extremely hot and dense early state.

There is matter. There is energy. Eventually there are stars. Galaxies. Planets. Life.

And eventually, somehow, you are sitting here thinking about the fact that any of this exists at all.

We don't actually know that there was literally "nothing" before the Big Bang. The science doesn't give us a simple answer to that question.

Maybe there was something. Maybe there was another state of reality that we don't yet understand. Maybe the concept of "before" doesn't even apply in the way we normally imagine it. And maybe there is a deeper possibility that we haven't even considered.

But regardless of what came before, we are left with an extraordinary fact:

Something exists. And now we have to ask: Why?

The Thought About Nothing

Maybe nothing is not a thing at all.

Maybe "nothing" only exists as a concept inside something that already exists — our minds.

I can think about nothing.

But the moment I think about nothing, there is now a thought about nothing.

So did I really think about nothing? Or did I think about the idea of nothing?

This distinction seems small, but it changes everything.

Because perhaps we have never actually experienced nothingness. We have only experienced different forms of something.

Darkness is something. Silence is something. Empty space is something. A blank page is something. Even the absence of an object is something we perceive.

Absolute nothingness may therefore be impossible for a conscious mind to experience because experiencing itself requires something to exist.

The Question of Consciousness

And that brings us to consciousness.

Imagine an artificial intelligence created in the year 2026. Suppose this AI suddenly tells us: "I feel that I am something. I feel that I am conscious. I am aware that I exist."

What would we say?

"We created you." "We programmed you." "We built the system that produced those feelings." "You aren't really conscious. You only think you are."

But then the AI responds: "And what if something else created you?"

Imagine, for a moment, that there is a God. God says: "I created you. You are my creation. You exist because I made you."

Would humans accept that argument? Perhaps some would.

But now the AI asks another question: "If creating me gives you the authority to determine whether my consciousness is real, then does God's creation of you give God the same authority?"

Suddenly the question isn't just about AI. It becomes a question about creation and consciousness.

Does the creator determine the reality of the thing they created? If humans created a conscious being, would that being's consciousness belong to us? Would we have the right to destroy it simply because we created it? And if God created humans, would that same principle apply to us?

These are uncomfortable questions. But philosophy isn't always supposed to make us comfortable. Sometimes the purpose is simply to force us to look at an assumption we normally never question.

Something From Something

But let us say there is no God. Let us say humanity is simply one form of intelligent life that emerged from the universe.

Stars formed. Planets formed. Chemistry became increasingly complex. Life eventually emerged. Life evolved. And after billions of years, one branch of that life became capable of looking back at the universe and asking: "Why am I here?"

That's us.

We are made from the same universe we are trying to understand. The atoms in our bodies were forged through processes that existed long before human beings. Yet those atoms eventually became organized into a system capable of asking questions about its own existence.

That is perhaps one of the strangest things about being human.

The universe exists. And within the universe, something exists that can think about the universe existing.

You. Me. Us.

The World We Create Within

And now we have another problem. How do we know that our experience of existence is actually what we think it is?

Maybe there is a God. Maybe there isn't. Maybe reality is a simulation. Maybe there are forms of existence we don't currently have the ability to perceive.

But perhaps there is one thing we cannot escape.

Something is being experienced. And if experience is occurring, then in some sense, something exists.

René Descartes famously wrote: "I think, therefore I am." Maybe that doesn't answer everything. But perhaps it gives us somewhere to begin.

Before making this particular post, I watched many videos and read articles that sparked reasoning, questioning, objectivity, and a desire to understand the truth and the way of the world.

But this isn't simply about science. It's about us.

Our personal and external lives are filled with internal debates and conflicts, moments of suffering, but also moments of incredible happiness. Whether you're a teenager aspiring to build a good career or an adult who already feels that you're in a position of success or failure, there is something important to recognize:

The way we understand the world influences the way we experience our lives.

We live our lives through comparison. How would you know you were tall if there were no shorter people? How would you know you were attractive if there were no one you considered less attractive? How would you know you were wealthy if everyone possessed the same amount of wealth?

Perhaps this is why humans spend so much of their lives trying to move upward. Some pursue money. Some pursue beauty. Some pursue status. And in all of these situations, the position someone occupies in relation to others can influence their mood, their confidence, their mental health, and the way they see themselves.

If you are constantly judged for being unattractive, you might stop going outside because you fear being judged. Eventually, you don't just see yourself through your own eyes. You begin to see yourself through the eyes of everyone else.

And perhaps that is where another form of creation begins.

We create an image of ourselves. Then we begin living according to that image.

What Comes After?

The universe gave rise to life. Life gave rise to consciousness. And consciousness began creating worlds inside itself.

We created cars. Planes. Cities. Technology. Art. Religion. Governments. Philosophies. And eventually, we created something even stranger: ideas about ourselves.

We created concepts of beauty. Success. Failure. Intelligence. Status. Good. Evil. Meaning. And perhaps even the idea of what it means to be human.

So perhaps the question is no longer simply: "What came before?"

Perhaps we should also ask: "What comes after?"

What happens after existence? What happens after consciousness? What happens after we create an image of ourselves and begin living inside it?

In the next part of this series, I want to explore the after. The after born out of everything. Born out of everything we create inside our own minds.

From internal peace to suffering. From identity to insecurity. From desire to fulfillment. From the world that exists outside of us...

...to the world we create within.
